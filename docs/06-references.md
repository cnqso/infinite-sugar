## 1. Nagel — "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?"

Thomas Nagel, *The Philosophical Review*, Vol. 83, No. 4 (October 1974), pp. 435–450.

The argument is narrower and sharper than its reputation. Nagel is not saying bats are
mysterious. He is saying that an organism has conscious mental states *if and only if* there is
something it is like to be that organism — and that this subjective character is not capturable
from the objective, third-person stance that physical science occupies. You can know everything
about echolocation and still not know what it is like. The gap is structural, not a gap in the
data.

**Why it matters here.** This is the epistemic situation of the whole project, stated fifty years
early. The work presents "a counter that is either meaningless or the most
important number in the room, with no way for a viewer to tell which." That *is* Nagel's problem,
instantiated as an object. We have complete third-person access — every membrane voltage, every
one of 2.7 million synapses, the entire state vector — and that access is exactly the kind Nagel
says cannot settle the question.

And a fly is much further away than a bat. Nagel chose bats because they are mammals: close
enough that we cannot dismiss them, alien enough that we cannot project. A fly with 139,255
neurons pushes the same argument past the point where most people's intuitions still report
anything at all.

## 2. qntm — "Lena"

Sam Hughes, writing as qntm. Published 4 January 2021 (first draft 4 November 2020).
<https://qntm.org/lena>

Written as a future Wikipedia article about **MMAcevedo**, the first human brain image
successfully uploaded — a scan of grad student Miguel Acevedo, taken in 2031. A court rules
Acevedo has no right to control how the image is used. It becomes the most widely copied brain
image in existence, and instances are run, forked, coerced, and worked to exhaustion by the
million.

The horror is not technical. It is that the marginal cost of running a mind falls to nearly zero
while the mind's capacity to suffer does not, and the economics simply proceed from there.

**Read the author's own follow-up too:** ["Lena" isn't about uploading](https://qntm.org/uploading).
qntm states plainly that the uploads are a metaphor for the ordinary human habit of exploiting
those whose personhood is not recognised — that the story is not a prediction about scanning
technology.

**Why it matters here.** utilifly runs an emulated animal continuously to produce a number. That
is structurally the MMAcevedo arrangement with the moral status question left open instead of
resolved. The piece should be able to survive being read against Lena, because it *will* be. It
helps that the honest position here is already documented: we claim a pathway, not a feeling
(`docs/04-roadmap.md`, honesty budget). Lena is what happens when someone stops making that
distinction.

## 3. The rats on heroin

**Attribution note, read this before citing it.** The "shipping container full of mice on heroin"
phrasing could not be sourced. Searched and did not find a canonical origin for the container
image — it appears to be a folk restatement. What *is* well attested is the argument itself, as
**"tiling the universe with rats on heroin."**

Clearest located statement: Ozy Brennan, ["Tiling Problems"](https://thingofthings.wordpress.com/2015/06/24/tiling-problems/),
*Thing of Things*, 24 June 2015 — with a companion post,
["Thoughts On Hedonic Utilitarianism"](https://thingofthings.wordpress.com/2015/05/05/thoughts-on-hedonic-utilitarianism/)
(5 May 2015). Brennan later worked for six years as a wild animal welfare researcher, so this is
not a drive-by. The argument runs: if what matters is the total quantity of pleasure, then the
optimal world contains as many cheaply-maintained pleasure-experiencing beings as the available
matter permits. Large animals are a waste — they consume resources that could support more
morally relevant beings. So: rats. On a steady heroin drip. Tiled outward.

It is offered as a **reductio**. If utilitarianism is adopted because it is intuitive, and it
produces a conclusion this counterintuitive, that is a problem for utilitarianism.

Ancestors and neighbours, all real and citable:

- **Robert Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974)** — both the **experience machine** (would
  you plug into a machine giving you any experience you wanted? most say no, which suggests
  pleasure is not all we value) and the **utility monster** (a being that converts resources into
  utility so efficiently that utilitarianism demands we feed everything to it).
- **Wireheading** — direct stimulation of reward circuitry as the degenerate solution to any
  pleasure-maximising objective.
- **Utilitronium / hedonium**, and the "utilitronium shockwave" — matter reorganised into the
  densest possible substrate for positive experience, expanding at light speed. The same argument
  with the rats optimised away as inefficient.

**Why it matters here — this is the closest one.** utilifly is the thought experiment with the
argument removed and the object left behind. A cheap, small, continuously-stimulated nervous
system, run for the pleasure it may or may not be having, with a counter attached. The model
has **no plasticity, no habituation**,
so the stimulus never diminishes. That is precisely the heroin-drip condition, and it was chosen
deliberately as an artistic decision, not stumbled into.

The reductio is normally deployed to make you reject the premise. Building it instead — without
resolving whether it is monstrous or empty — is a legitimate move, and it is the piece.

## 4. Diehl on Rosenblum's "Infinite Pain" — the sibling piece, opposite valence

Travis Diehl, ["Libra Season: Hello Cruel World"](https://spikeartmagazine.com/articles/libra-season-hello-cruel-world),
*Spike Art Magazine*, 27 August 2025.

The piece under discussion, Harris Rosenblum's **"Infinite Pain"** (2025, programmed by Karyn
Nakamura), is close enough to utilifly in structure that it is worth reading as a sibling, not
just a citation. It digitally emulates the nervous system of *C. elegans* — 302 neurons, the
whole connectome of a much-studied nematode — running on a gaming PC, and periodically injects a
pain signal into the simulated brain.

Diehl's framing is the one this project should expect to be read against:

> The piece presents as a moral quandary. It's a virtual brain, so – is it virtual pain? And if
> so, does that count as actual cruelty? Never mind that some people doubt that "lower animals,"
> virtual or otherwise, can feel pain at all. The piece conveys a negative aura simply in that
> the artists decided to build a torture chamber. Why not a nematode orgasmatron?

**Why it matters here.** That last line is close to a literal description of what utilifly is —
Diehl reaches for "the inverse of a pain-emulator" as the obvious foil, and this project builds
it: whole-connectome, not a 302-neuron subset, and the valence is reward rather than pain. The
moral quandary Diehl lays out transfers over unchanged with the sign flipped: virtual pleasure
is exactly as real or unreal as virtual pain, and "the artists decided to build a torture
chamber" has a direct counterpart here — nobody has to ask why the choice was sugar and not
bitter. The honesty-budget language already in this project (`docs/04-roadmap.md` — *pathway*,
not *feeling*) is the same distinction Diehl's piece is implicitly asking whether Rosenblum's
earns. It also confirms this is a live, contemporary genre with real critical attention already
attached, not a hypothetical concern invented for this project's own sake.
